You push your first piece of code, ten minutes later, your senior leaves fourteen comments on it, and your stomach drops. If you are a fresher or about to join, in the next five minutes, I will explain what a code review really is. The three ways freshers react badly to review comments, and a simple routine that makes seniors trust you faster. Stay till the end, because the third mistake is the one that quietly slows down your first year. First, what is a code review? Before your code goes into the main project, at least one other person reads it. They check if it works, if it is easy to read, and if it follows the team's rules. In most teams, this happens on a pull request, which is just a request that says, "Please check my changes and merge them." Here is the part nobody tells you. Comments are not a marks sheet. Your senior is not judging you. They are protecting the project, and every senior has had fourteen comments on their own first pull request. Now the three mistakes. Mistake number one, taking every comment as an attack. A comment like, "Why not use a constant here?" is a question about the code, not about you. If you reply with a long defence or go silent for two days, the review drags on. Read each comment slowly and ask yourself one thing. What is this person trying to protect? Mistake number two, fixing without understanding. You get a comment, you change the line exactly as told, and you push again. It works today, but next week you make the same mistake in another file, and the same comment comes back. If you do not understand a comment, ask, Write, I changed this, but can you help me understand why this is better?" That one line shows you are learning, and seniors love that. Mistake number three, replying to nothing, you fix the code, but you never answer the comments. So the reviewer has to open every line again to find out what changed. Always reply, write done, or fixed in the latest commit. Or, "I kept this as it is, because of this reason." If you disagree, say it politely with a reason. Disagreeing is fine, ignoring is not. So here is the routine that works. Step one, before you raise a pull request, read your own changes once, line by line, as if someone else wrote them. You will catch many small things yourself. Step two, keep the pull request small, a review of twenty lines, gets done in an hour. A review of two thousand lines sits for days. Step three, when comments come, first read all of them without replying. Then group them, then fix them one by one, and reply to each. Step four, keep a personal list of the comments you get. After a month, you will see the same three or four patterns, and fixing those makes your next reviews almost empty. A small example, say your senior writes, "This function does too many things. Do not just rename it. Ask "Would you like me to split it into two smaller functions?" That shows you understood the idea, and it saves one more round of review, one more thing. If a comment is unclear or sounds harsh in text, do not argue in the chat. Ask for five minutes on a call. Most of the time, the tone you imagined was never there. So to sum up, a review is not a verdict. Do not take it personally, do not fix blindly, and always reply to every comment. Read your own code first, keep changes small, and learn from the patterns.
