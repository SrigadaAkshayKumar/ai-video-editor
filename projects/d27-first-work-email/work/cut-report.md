## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

Your first work email can make your manager trust you, or quietly worry about you, and most freshers never learn the rules because nobody teaches them. This video is for every fresher who is about to join, or just join, a tech company. By the end, you will know the four email mistakes that make a new joiner look careless, and a simple template you can copy for your very first message. First, why this matters? In a campus, you talk to people face-to-face. In a company, especially with a team in another city or another country, almost everything happens in writing. Your manager may never see how hard you work. They see your emails, your messages, and your status updates. So, your writing becomes your reputation, and here is the catch. Nobody will correct you politely. They will just form an opinion. Let us start with the mistake that almost every fresher makes in the very first week. Mistake number one, a subject line that says nothing. High query, doubt, urgent. Your manager gets many emails a day, and a vague subject goes to the bottom. Write a subject that tells the whole story in a few words, for example, access request for the project repository needed by Friday. Mistake number two, writing one long paragraph with the ask hidden at the end. Put the ask in the first two lines, then give two or three lines of background. Then say what you have already tried. A manager who sees that you tried first will help you faster and respect you more. Mistake number three is the one that makes people stop trusting freshers. Stay with me for it. Mistake number three, using reply all or adding people without thinking. Imagine you ask a small question and it goes to 40 people, including senior leaders. Or worse, you forward a mail with a private comment further down the chain. Before you press send, check three things. Who is in the two line? Who is in the copy line? And what is written in the older messages below? If your question is only for your team lead, send it only to your team lead. A small rule that saves you from embarrassment. Write the email first and add the recipients last. Mistake number four, and the most damaging one, staying silent when you are stuck or late. A fresher gets a task, spends two days stuck, says nothing, and then the deadline passes. The problem was never the task, the problem was the silence. Here is a simple habit. When you are blocked, write a short update. What you were asked to do, what is done, what is blocking you. And when you will have it finished or what help you need. Four lines sent early. Managers forgive a slow fresher who tells them early. They do not forgive a fresher who surprises them. Now let me give you a template you can use for your first message to a manager or a team lead. Start with a clear subject, such as question on task one, need your input today, line one, greet them by name. Line two, say what you were asked to do and what you have done so far. Line three, say exactly what you need from them and by when. Line four, thank them. Keep the whole email under 10 lines. Before you send it, read it once out loud. If any sentence sounds rude or confusing to you, it will sound worse to them. So to sum up, write a subject that says something. Put the ask first. Check who is on the mail before you send it and never go silent when you are stuck. Now I want to hear from you.
