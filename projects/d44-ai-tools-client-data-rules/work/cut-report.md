## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

Passing one error message into a chatbot can end a freezer's job. Not because the error was wrong, but because of what was inside it. This video is for every freezer who is about to join a software company or has just joined. By the end, you will know the three things you must never paste into an AI tool. The safe way to still use AI at work and the one question to ask your manager in the first week. Let me start with a real story. In 2023, engineers at Samsung pasted their own confidential source code and meeting notes into a public chatbot to get quick help. The tool could keep what people typed in. Samsung then banned public AI tools on company devices. This year, it was reported that Samsung is bringing such tools back, but only behind a security layer and after training. Look at that last part. Even a giant company did not say AI is bad. It said use it, but only with rules. Your company has the same rules. They are just written in a document most freshers never read, never paste number one, anything that belongs to the client. In a service company, the code, the database tables, the screenshots, and even the error logs usually belong to the client, not to you and not to your company. Logs are the sneaky part. A log line can hold a customer name, a phone number, or a server address. The client contract almost always has a confidentiality clause. Breaking it is not a small mistake. It can mean a security incident report with your name on it in your first year. Never paste number two, secrets. Paste needles, access keys, tokens, connection strings. Many freshers copy a whole configuration file and ask the chatbot, "Why is this not working?" That file may contain the keys to a live system. And here is the catch. The same rule applies to public code websites. If you upload client code to your personal profile, even for practice, that is also a leak. Keep your practice projects fully separate from office work. Never paste number three, internal documents. Meeting notes, project plan, emails from your manager, even the salary bansheet. People paste these to get a quick summary. It feels harmless. But it is the same thing that happened in the story I told you. Now, you may be thinking, "Then, how do I use AI at all?" Good question. That is the next part, and it is the part that actually helps your career. Here is the safe way. Four steps. One, ask your manager or the onboarding mail which AI tools are approved here. Many companies now give an approved tool with a security layer. Use only that one for office work. Two, even in an approved tool, remove names, numbers, and keys before you paste. Three, ask general questions. Instead of pasting the client's function, ask, "How do I read a file line by line in Java?" Learn the idea, then write the code yourself. Four, always check the answer. If you cannot explain a line of code, do not submit it. A reviewer will ask you, and sooner or later in the interview for your next job, someone will ask you too. So, to sum up, never paste client data, secrets, or internal documents into a public AI tool. Use only the approved tool and ask general questions. In your first week, ask this one question, "What is the policy for AI tools on this project?" It takes 10 seconds, and it shows you are careful.
