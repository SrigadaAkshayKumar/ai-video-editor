## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

Most freshers think a timesheet is just a boring form. Then, one wrong entry makes a client build the wrong hours, and suddenly, a manager is asking why. This video is for every fresher who has joined, or is about to join, a software services company. By the end, you will know the four timesheet mistakes that hurt freshers most, and the one that is the easiest to avoid. First, why does it matter at all? In a services company, the client often pays for the time of the people working on the project. Your timesheet is how that time is counted. So, it is not only for your manager, it feeds billing, project cost, and sometimes your own utilization, which means how much of your time was used on project work. Here is the catch. Freshers who are still in training, or waiting for a project, usually have a separate code for that time. Using the wrong code can make your time look like build work, or make real work look like idle time. Remember, every company names these codes differently. Ask what yours are. Think of a simple example. Ravi joins on Monday, spends two weeks in training, and then gets a project. If he writes project work for those two weeks, the record is wrong from day one, and fixing it later takes far more effort than writing it right today. Mistake number one, filling the timesheet once a month from memory. By then, you forget what you did on which day, and the numbers become guesses. Fill it every day, even if it takes only two minutes before you log off. Mistake number two, copying your neighbor's entries. A friend on another project may use different codes, different tasks, and different rules. What is right for them can be wrong for you. If you are not sure, ask your team lead, not the person sitting next to you. And the third mistake is the one that quietly worries managers the most. I will come to it in a moment. Mistake number three, putting hours that you did not work. Maybe you were stuck on a bug, or you were waiting for access, or you were learning something new. It feels bad to write that, so you write coding for eight hours. Do not do this. Wrong hours are a record, and records get checked. If the client questions a bill, your entries are the evidence. Write what really happened in plain words. For example, waiting for access to the test environment. That tells your manager there is a blocker, and blockers get fixed. A padded entry hides the problem, and the problem stays. Honest entries protect you. Padded ones can come back later when you least expect it. Mistake number four, missing the deadline. Most companies have a day by which timesheets must be submitted and approved. Miss it, and you may get reminder mails, a delay in approval, or a talk with your manager. In some places, it can even affect other processes tied to your account. So, here is what to do this week. One, ask your team lead for the timesheet deadline and the code list. Two, set a daily reminder on your phone 10 minutes before you log off. Three, write each entry in plain words with the real task. And four, if a day went on training or waiting, use the code for that and do not hide it. If you do these four things, you will never think about your timesheet again, and that is the whole point. Let me recap. Fill it daily, not monthly. Do not copy a friend. Never pad the hours, and know your deadline. Here is my question for you guys.
