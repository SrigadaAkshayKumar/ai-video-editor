## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

Ravi joined his first tech job on a Monday. By Thursday, his manager had already formed an opinion about him, and Ravi had no idea. This video is for every fresher who is about to join or has just joined a software company. By the end, you will know the five small mistakes that make a new joiner look careless in the first week, and exactly what to do instead. Stay for Mistake #4, because it is the one that quietly follows people for months. First, why does the first week matter so much? Because in a big company, your manager and your team do not know you yet. They have no past work to judge, so they judge what they can see. Do you reply to emails? Do you reach on time? Do you finish what you start? None of this needs talent, it needs habits, and habits are exactly what you can fix before day one. Here is the pattern, problem, why it happens, and what to do. Let us go through all five Mistake #1, ignoring emails from the onboarding team. Your first week will bring many emails from HR, from the Tech Help Desk, from your learning team. Somehow deadlines, a mail about uploading a document or finishing a compulsory course may be marked due in two days. If you miss it, it reaches your manager as a reminder. What to do? Check your workmail at the start of the day, at lunch and before you leave. Reply to anything that asks for action, even with just a line saying "Received, I will finish it today." Mistake #2, not finishing the compulsory courses and the time sheet. Most companies have online courses on security and conduct and a system to record your time. The details differ from company to company, but the rule is the same, do them on time. A fresher who finishes them in week one looks organized, one who is chased for them looks careless. Mistake #3, staying silent when you are stuck. Freshers think if I ask too many questions, they will think I am weak, so they struggle alone for a full day on something a teammate could explain in five minutes. Here is a simple rule many managers like, try for 30 minutes, write down what you tried, then ask with your notes. A question that says I tried this and this and I am stuck here shows effort. A question that says it is not working shows nothing. Silence is not seen as strength, it is seen as risk because nobody knows when you are stuck until it is too late. Now mistake #4, the one I promised promising a date and missing it without telling anyone. Ravi told his team lead he would finish a small task by Wednesday evening. On Wednesday he was stuck, said nothing and sent it on Friday. The task was small, but what the lead remembered was not the task, it was that Ravi's word could not be trusted. In office language, this is called managing expectations. If you will be late, say so before the deadline, not after. One message early is enough. Something like, I am blocked on this I need until Friday. Is that fine? Late work is forgiven. Silent late work is remembered. Mistake #5, treating office rules as optional. Every company has rules about work from office days, leave, working hours and dress. These differ a lot between companies and even between teams and they change. So never rely on what a friend in another company told you. On your first day, ask your manager or your buddy three things. Which days must I be in the office? How do I apply for leave and whom do I inform? What are the working hours for this team? Then follow them for the first month even if others seem relaxed. Seniors have earned their flexibility. You are still building yours. So to sum up, check your mail, finish courses and time sheets, ask with notes one early when you will be late and learn the office rules on day one. None of it needs talent. All of it gets you noticed for the right reasons. Now tell me, which of these five do you think you are most likely to make?
