## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

It is your second month, you run one command, and a minute later, your lead asks in the team chat, "Why is the test server down?" Your stomach drops because you know it was you. If you are a fresher in your first tech job, this will happen to you in some form. In the next five minutes, I will give you the exact four steps to follow in the first hour and the one thing that turns a small mistake into a serious problem. Stay till the end because the fourth step is the one managers remember. First, understand what actually goes wrong in these situations. The mistake itself is usually not the problem. Everyone breaks something sooner or later. The problem is the time between the mistake and the moment your team finds out. Here is a story. Ravi deleted the wrong folder from a shared drive on a Friday evening. He was scared, so he said nothing and hoped nobody would notice. On Monday morning, the team lost two hours looking for the files and then somebody checked the activity log. The deleted folder was a small problem. Hiding it was the big one. So remember this line, a mistake that you report is an incident. A mistake that you hide becomes a question about your honesty. Now the four steps. Step one, stop. Do not run another command to fix your first command. When people panic, they type faster and the second mistake is often worse than the first. Take your hands off the keyboard for 10 seconds and write down in your notes exactly what you ran and when. Step two, tell your lead or your buddy right away. Do not wait until you have a full solution. Send a short message in plain words and I will give you a template in a moment. If the problem is live, meaning real users or a client are affected, speak up even faster. Because your team may need to roll back a change or switch to a backup and they can only do that if they know. Here is a message you can copy. I made a mistake. At 10, 15, I ran this command on this system. The effect is that this service is not responding. I have not done anything else since. I think this could be rolled back, but I want to check with you first. Can you help me or tell me who should look at it? Notice what this message has. What you did when you did it, what the effect is, what you have not touched and a request for help. It has no long apology and no excuses. Seniors read a message like this and relax because it gives them everything they need to start fixing. Step 3. Help to fix it but do not play hero. Answer questions, honestly, share your notes and your screen and do exactly what your senior asks. Do not try a clever recovery on your own, especially on shared systems, client data or anything in production. And now the step that managers remember, step 4. Help to make sure it cannot happen again. Many good teams hold what is called a "blameless post-mortem". Google's engineering book describes it as a written record of what happened. The impact the actions taken, the cause and the follow-up actions. The key idea is "blameless". The focus is on how the system allows the mistake, not on punishing a person, because when people fear blame, they hide problems. So after the fix, offer one improvement, maybe a confirmation prompt before deleting a checklist, a separate test environment or a note in the team wiki. A fresher who says "here is how we can prevent this next time" is a fresher who gets trusted with more work. Now the traps. Do not delete logs or edit history to cover your tracks. Do not blame the tool, the instructions or a teammate in your first message. And do not message only on a personal chat because the incident belongs in the official team channel. One more point. Every company has its own incident process and some mistakes like leaking client data or customer information must go to the security or compliance team, not only your lead. If you are not sure, ask your lead in the very first message which team needs to know. Honest mistakes made while following your task are treated very differently from hiding, lying or breaking a clear rule. So your best protection is always the same. Speak early, speak plainly. So to sum up, in the first hour, stop, tell your lead, help to fix it with your senior and then suggest how to prevent it. Do these four and a mistake becomes a story about how you handle pressure.
