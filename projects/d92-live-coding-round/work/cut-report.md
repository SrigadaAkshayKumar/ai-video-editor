## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

You can write a coding answer that does not run and still gets selected, and you can write code that runs perfectly and still get rejected. The difference is what you do in the first two minutes. If you are a fresher facing a live coding round in a video call or in a room with an interviewer watching, stay with me. By the end, you will know three steps that score marks even when your code fails and the one habit that quietly rejects good students. First, understand what is happening on the other side of the screen. For a fresher, the interviewer does not expect a perfect solution. They have seen your resume, so they already know you can pass exams. In a live round, they are checking three things. Can you understand a problem? Can you think in small steps? And can you explain your thinking to another person? That last one is what you will actually work on every day in your first job. Nobody codes alone in silence. So, a student who talks clearly through an average solution often beats a silent student who types a better one. Now, the three steps, step one, do not start typing. Repeat the problem in your own words and ask two or three questions. For example, you can say, so I have an array of numbers and I need to find two numbers that add up to a target. Can the array be empty? Can numbers repeat? Is it sorted? This does three things. It proves you listened. It stops you from solving the wrong problem. And it buys you thinking time without any silence that feels awkward. Then, say one small example out loud with the expected answer. If your example and the interviewer's example do not match, you have just saved yourself 10 minutes of wrong work. But here is the catch. Most freshers do this step and then fall into the trap in step two. Step two. Say the brute force answer first even if it is slow. Brute force means the simple obvious way. For the two numbers problem, it is check every pair of numbers. Say it plainly and say why it is slow. For a long list, checking every pair takes too much time. Now many students skip this because they think the interviewer wants the clever answer immediately. So they go silent for five minutes searching for the clever trick. That is the habit I promised you. Silence is what rejects good students, not a wrong answer. When you say the simple answer first, two things happen. You already have a working solution on the table and the interviewer will usually give you a hint towards the better one. Like, can you avoid checking every pair? Take that hint happily. Nobody is counting hints against a fresher. They are watching how you use them. Step three. Before you say done, test your own code. Take the small example from step one and walk through your code line by line with the values changing. Then try one edge case, an empty input, a single item or a repeated value. If you find a bug yourself, say so Kalmin. I see a problem here. Let me fix it. This is a good moment, not a bad one. In a real job, a developer who tests before sharing is the one manager's trust. And if time runs out before your code is complete, do not freeze. Say what is left. For example, I would handle the empty case here and then return the result explaining unfinished work clearly still shows how you think. One more trap. Do not copy a memorized solution and rush through it. Interviewers often change one small condition to check if you understood or only remembered. So here is what to do this week. Pick anyone easy problem. Set a timer for 20 minutes. Speak out loud while you solve it, even if you are alone in your room, even if it feels silly. Say the problem. Ask your questions. Say the simple answer, then improve it. Then test it. If you can do it once with a friend who plays the interviewer, that one practice session is worth more than watching 10 solution videos.
