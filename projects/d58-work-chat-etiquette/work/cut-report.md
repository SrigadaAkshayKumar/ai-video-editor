## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

Your manager will form an opinion about you from a message that is only one word long. That word is hi. If you are a fresher about to start your first job or already in your first month, this video is for you. In the next five minutes, I will explain the three chat mistakes that quietly hurt freshers and the exact message to send instead. Stay till the end because the third mistake is the one that can actually get you into trouble. First, why this matters? In many companies, especially with hybrid work, most of your day happens in a chat tool. Your manager may not sit near you, your teammates may be in another city, so your messages are the way they meet you. They see how clearly you think, how much you respect their time, and whether you can be trusted with work. A fresher who writes clearly looks ready even before the code is ready. The good news is that this is a skill, and you can learn it today. Mistake number one. The lonely hi, you send hi, sir, and then you wait. Or you write hi, are you free, and wait again. Your manager is now stuck. If they are busy, they have to reply just to ask what you want. A two line problem becomes a 10-minute conversation. The fix is to send the whole message in one go, say hello, and then give the question with its context. Here is a sample. Hi, I am working on the login ticket. I tried restarting the service and checking the logs, and I still get the same error. Could you spare five minutes today to point me in the right direction? See what happened there? Your manager knows the topic, knows you already tried something, and knows exactly how much time you need. They can answer even from their phone. Mistake number two. Sending the right message in the wrong place. Some freshers send every doubt privately to one senior. That person slowly becomes your helpline, and they get tired. Others do the opposite. They post a personal or half-angry message in the whole team channel where fifty people can read it. Here is a simple rule. Questions that other people may also have, ask in the team channel. Others will learn from the answer, and you will not be a burden on one person. Anything about your leave, your performance, or a complaint, take it to your manager privately, or in a call. And watch the clock. Many people mute chat after work hours. A message at eleven at night is not urgent for anyone but you. Write it, and schedule it for the morning if your tool allows it. Unless something is truly broken, it can wait. Mistake number three. The careless message. This is the one I promised. Work chat is not a private diary. Companies can usually look at messages sent on their own tools. So a joke about your manager, a complaint about the client, or a screenshot of the project sent to a friend can come back to you later. Be especially careful with client names, code, passwords, and anything marked confidential. Never paste them into a personal chat or a personal account. If you are not sure, ask your manager before you share. Also be careful when you are annoyed. Never reply to feedback while you are angry, read it, wait ten minutes, and then write a calm answer. A reply like, "Thanks for the feedback, I will fix this and update you by evening," makes you look mature. And one more small habit. When someone sends you a task, reply quickly with a simple, "Okay, I will do it." Silence makes people nervous. So here is what to do from today. Before you send any message, check four things. Does it have the topic? Does it say what you already tried? Does it say what you need? And is it going to the right place, at the right time? Quick recap. Mistake one, the lonely hi.
