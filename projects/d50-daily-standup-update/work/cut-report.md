## Clean script (what the viewer will hear)

Your first daily stand-up lasts about two minutes for you, and in those two minutes, your manager decides if you are on track or if you are the person who always has a problem. If you are a fresher about to join a tech company, this video is for you. By the end, you will know the three-part answer that works every single day and the one sentence that can save you when you are completely stuck. Stay till the end, because the third mistake is the one that quietly hurts freshers the most. First, what is a stand-up? Most software teams work in short cycles called sprints, usually two weeks long. Every working day, the team meets for a quick call at the same time to share progress. The official Scrum Guide describes this meeting called the Daily Scrum as a 15-minute event. Its purpose is simple. Check progress toward the sprint goal and spot problems early. So, it is not a report to the manager, it is a quick sync with your teammates. That is why long stories do not work here. And here is the catch. 15 minutes for the whole team means very little time for each person. So, how you speak matters more than how much you speak. Here is the answer that works every day. Three parts. Yesterday today, blockers, part 1 yesterday. Say what you finished, not how hard you tried. For example, I completed the login form validation and raised it for review. Part 2 today, say what you will do in one or two clear tasks. For example, today I will fix the review comments and start the next ticket. Part 3, blockers, say what is stopping you or say no blockers. Never skip this part. Keep the whole answer under 30 seconds. Write it in your notes before the call in three short lines. Nobody will mind that you are reading and you will never go blank. Now, the three mistakes. Mistake number one, saying worked on the same task for three days. To a manager, that sounds like no progress. Instead, break the task into small steps and report the step. I finished reading the code and today I will write the test. Mistake number two, telling the whole story. Refers often explain every error message, every search, every idea they tried. The team has other work to do. Give the result in one line. If a long discussion is needed, say I will discuss this with you after the call. Can we speak for five minutes? That is a professional sentence and it keeps the meeting short. Mistake number three, the silent blocker. You are stuck on day one. You say no blockers because you feel shy and on day three, you are still stuck. Now, the problem is bigger and everyone is surprised. A blocker is not a confession. The stand-up exists for exactly this. Your team can only help with what they hear. So, when you are stuck, use this sentence. I am blocked on this task. I have tried these two things. Can someone help me after the call? It shows effort. It is specific and it asks for help without drama. If the real problem is that you do not understand the task at all, say that too, but say it to your lead right after the call in a message or a short chat. Here is what to do before your first stand-up. Write your three lines yesterday, today, blockers every morning. Use the ticket name or number so people know exactly what you mean. Join the call two minutes early with your microphone tested and your camera on if the team keeps it on. Listen to what others say. You will learn the project names, the tools and who to ask for what all in 15 minutes. And if your answer changes during the day, send a short update in the team chat. Managers love freshers who communicate early. So, to sum up, use three parts yesterday, today, blockers. Keep it under 30 seconds. Report steps, not stories and never stay silent when you are blocked. Now, I want to hear from you.
